Step 0 · 3 minutes

What fine-tuning is, in one sentence

One definition, one analogy, and the single most important thing fine-tuning does not do.

Fine-tuning means taking a model that has already been trained and continuing its training on your own examples, so that its behaviour shifts toward those examples.

Every large language model starts with pre-training: it reads an enormous pile of text and learns to predict the next word, over and over, for weeks on thousands of chips. The result is called the base model. Everything the model "knows" is stored in its weights (also called parameters): billions of numbers that decide how it turns input text into output text. Fine-tuning nudges those numbers a little, using your examples, so the model's habits change.

Analogy

A fully qualified general doctor starts a specialist residency in dermatology. They do not relearn medicine from scratch. They already know anatomy, chemistry and how to talk to patients. The residency adjusts what they do: how they examine skin, which questions they ask first, how they write up a case. Fine-tuning is that residency. The base model is the general doctor.

Base model

General ability, learned in pre-training from public text.

+

Your examples

Hundreds or thousands of "input, ideal output" pairs.

→

Fine-tuned model

Same general ability, new habits: your format, your tone, your task.

What fine-tuning changes, and what it does not

Changes reliably

  • Style and tone (formal, terse, friendly)
  • Output format (always valid JSON, always three bullet points)
  • Task habits (always ask for the order number first)
  • Which of several possible behaviours the model prefers

Does not change reliably

  • Facts. A few hundred examples do not turn a model into a database.
  • Knowledge of things that happened after pre-training
  • Anything private to one user or one request
  • Deep reasoning ability (it can be sharpened, not created)
Key idea

Fine-tuning teaches how to answer, not what is true. If your problem is "the model does not know my company's return policy", the fix is usually retrieval (Part 3), not training. If your problem is "the model knows enough but keeps answering in the wrong shape", fine-tuning is the right tool.

Common doubt

"So does the fine-tuned model forget what it learned in pre-training?" Mostly no, if you do it carefully. The nudges are small compared with the original training. But it is possible to push too hard and damage general ability. Step 6 shows how to check for that.

Step 1 · The decision

Prompting, RAG, or fine-tuning?

Three ways to change a model's behaviour. Learn which to reach for first and why fine-tuning is the last one, not the first.

When a model does not behave the way you want, you have three levers. Prompting means changing the instructions and examples you send with each request (Part 2). RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) means looking up relevant documents at request time and pasting them into the prompt so the model can read them (Part 3). Fine-tuning means changing the model itself.

LeverGood forCostTime to changeMain risk
PromptingInstructions, a few examples, tone, simple formatsAlmost freeSeconds. Edit text, retry.Long prompts are re-sent (and billed, per token, the word-pieces a model reads) on every call; brittle for complex formats
RAGFacts that change, private documents, citing sourcesBuilding a search index; extra tokens per callMinutes. Update the documents.Retrieval returns the wrong passage; the model ignores it
Fine-tuningConsistent style and format at scale; teaching a small model a narrow task; cutting prompt lengthData work, training runs, sometimes hardwareHours to days. Retrain and re-check.Overfitting, forgetting, stale behaviour baked in

The order matters. Prompting is cheap to try and cheap to undo. RAG solves knowledge problems. Fine-tuning solves behaviour problems that prompting cannot fix, or that are too expensive to fix with a long prompt on every call.

Common doubt

"Will fine-tuning teach the model my company's facts?" Mostly no. A few hundred examples can teach the model to sound like it knows your policies, which is worse than not knowing, because it will confidently make them up. To give a model facts, put the facts in the prompt at request time, which is exactly what RAG does. Fine-tuning and RAG combine well: RAG supplies the facts, fine-tuning supplies the manners.

Key idea

Reach for the levers in order: prompt, then retrieve, then train. Each step costs more and is harder to undo than the one before. Fine-tuning is worth it when the cheaper levers have been tried honestly and fallen short.

Go deeper: the "long prompt tax"

Suppose your prompt needs twelve worked examples to get the format right, and you handle two million requests a month. Those examples are sent, and paid for, on every single call. A fine-tuned model that has absorbed the format needs a prompt one tenth the size. At high volume, the training cost pays for itself in saved tokens and faster responses. At low volume it never does. This arithmetic is the most common good reason to fine-tune.

Step 2 · The menu

Four kinds of fine-tuning

"Fine-tuning" is a family, not one technique. Each member needs a different kind of data and changes a different thing.

Supervised fine-tuning SFT

You show the model pairs of "input, ideal output". It learns to produce outputs like yours. This is what most people mean by fine-tuning, and it is where beginners should start.

data: prompt → ideal answer

Preference tuning DPO / RLHF

You show the model two answers to the same input and mark which is better. It learns your taste, not just your format. Used after SFT to polish judgement: politeness, safety, brevity.

data: prompt, better answer, worse answer

Continued pre-training rare

You feed the model a large pile of raw text from your field (medical notes, legal filings, a programming language) with no question-and-answer structure. It soaks up vocabulary and some knowledge. Expensive and easy to get wrong.

data: millions of words of raw text

Distillation cheaper

A big teacher model writes answers to many inputs. A small student model is fine-tuned on those answers. The student ends up nearly as good on that narrow task, but much faster and cheaper to run.

data: teacher's answers, used as SFT pairs

Four members of the fine-tuning family. Read the bottom line of each column: the data you need tells you which kind you are doing.

Analogy

Back to the doctor. SFT is watching a senior colleague handle fifty cases and copying their write-ups. Preference tuning is the senior colleague marking your write-ups: "this one is better, that one rambles". Continued pre-training is reading every dermatology textbook for a year. Distillation is the senior colleague writing model answers for a junior nurse to learn from, so the nurse can handle routine cases alone.

Go deeper: DPO versus RLHF

Both are ways to learn from preferences. RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback) first trains a separate "reward model" to score answers the way humans would, then trains the language model to chase high scores. It is powerful and fiddly. DPO (direct preference optimisation) skips the separate reward model and adjusts the language model directly from the better-versus-worse pairs. It is simpler to run and is the usual choice for small teams. For a beginner the practical difference is: DPO needs only a file of compared pairs; RLHF needs a whole extra training pipeline.

Key idea

Start with supervised fine-tuning. It needs the simplest data (input and ideal output), it is the best understood, and it covers most real needs. Add preference tuning only when SFT gets the format right but the judgement wrong.

Step 3 · Under the hood

Full fine-tuning versus adapters (LoRA)

You can move all the weights or just a few. Learn why almost everyone chooses "just a few", and what QLoRA adds.

Full fine-tuning updates every one of the model's billions of weights. It is the most flexible option and the most expensive: you need enough GPU memory (a GPU is the specialised chip that does the arithmetic of training and running models) to hold the whole model plus all the bookkeeping that training needs, which is several times the model's own size. For a large model that means a rack of high-end chips.

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) is the family of tricks that avoid this. The most popular is LoRA (low-rank adaptation). The idea: leave the base model exactly as it is (its weights are frozen, meaning they never change during training) and bolt on small trainable side-layers called adapters. Only the adapters learn. An adapter is typically well under one percent of the model's size, so training needs far less memory and produces a tiny file you can pass around.

Analogy

Full fine-tuning is rewriting a thick textbook to match your course. LoRA is covering the textbook in sticky notes. The book is untouched, the notes are small, and you can peel them off and stick on a different set for a different course. You can even hand the notes to a friend who owns the same book.

LoRA in one picture: the big block is read-only during training; the small adapters do all the learning. Different adapters can share the same base model.

QLoRA: the same trick on smaller hardware

Quantization means storing each weight with fewer bits, for example 4 bits instead of 16. The model gets several times smaller in memory and a little less precise. QLoRA loads the frozen base model in quantized form and trains normal LoRA adapters on top. Because the big block now takes a fraction of the memory, a model that once needed a server can be fine-tuned on a single mid-range GPU. The quality is usually close to plain LoRA, which is why QLoRA is the default choice for hobbyists and small teams.

MethodWhat movesMemory neededOutput fileWhen to choose
Full fine-tuningEvery weightVery high (many big GPUs)A whole new modelLarge budget, huge dataset, need maximum change

Key idea

Adapters make fine-tuning cheap, reversible and shareable. You keep one base model and a drawer full of small adapter files, one per task. If a fine-tune goes wrong, you delete a file, not a model.

Common doubt

"Is LoRA worse than full fine-tuning?" For style, format and narrow tasks, the difference is usually small enough that you cannot measure it. Full fine-tuning pulls ahead when you have a very large dataset and want deep changes, which is rarely the beginner's situation.

Go deeper: why "low-rank" works

Each layer of the model holds a large grid of numbers (a matrix). Full fine-tuning would change every entry. LoRA bets that the change you need is simple: it can be described as the product of two thin matrices, one tall and one wide, with far fewer numbers in total. "Rank" is the width of those thin matrices; a rank of 8 or 16 is common. It works because the adjustments a specialist needs are small and structured, not random noise across the whole grid.

Step 4 · The hard part

The data is the product

Training is a few commands. The dataset is where the weeks go, and where the quality comes from.

A fine-tuning dataset is a list of examples. For supervised fine-tuning each example is one prompt and the exact answer you wish the model had given. The most common file format is JSONL: a plain text file with one JSON object per line. It is popular because you can add examples by appending lines and inspect the file with any text editor.

# train.jsonl: one example per line, nothing else on the line
{"prompt": "Customer: My order arrived damaged.", "response": "I am sorry about that. Please reply with your order number and a photo, and we will send a replacement."}
{"prompt": "Customer: Where is my refund?", "response": "Refunds appear on your statement within 5 to 7 business days. Could you share the order number so I can check its status?"}

How many examples?

Rules of thumb, not laws. Tens of examples are enough to see whether the idea works at all. Hundreds usually produce a clear, measurable change in format and tone. Thousands are needed for robust behaviour across edge cases. Beyond that, quality and variety matter more than count. Five hundred careful, varied examples beat five thousand sloppy or repetitive ones every time.

Cover the edges

Angry customers, empty messages, two questions in one, questions in another language. If a case is missing from the data, the model will improvise on it.

Be consistent

If half the ideal answers start with "Hi" and half do not, the model learns to flip a coin. Decide the format once and apply it everywhere.

Remove the wrong ones

A single confidently wrong example teaches a confidently wrong habit. Contradictory pairs (same question, different answers) are worse than no example.

Keep responses honest

Include examples where the right answer is "I do not know, let me check". Otherwise the model learns that every question has a confident answer.

Synthetic data

Synthetic data is training data written by a model instead of a person, usually a large model asked to produce ideal answers or to invent varied customer questions. It is fast and cheap, and it is how distillation works. The risks: the big model's mistakes become your small model's habits; generated questions tend to be blander and less varied than real ones; and you may be limited by the big model's terms of use. The safe pattern is to generate, then have a person review a sample and delete the bad ones.

Licensing and privacy

Training data is copied into the model's habits. Do not train on customer messages that contain names, addresses or card numbers unless you have permission and have removed the personal details. Do not train on text you do not have the right to use. A fine-tuned model can and will repeat fragments of its training data.

Key idea

The model becomes a mirror of the dataset. Every inconsistency, every wrong answer and every missing case in the data will show up in the model's behaviour. Spend most of your fine-tuning time on the examples, not on the training settings.

Practical tip

Before training, split the data three ways. The training set (most of it) is what the model learns from. The validation set (a small held-out slice) is used during training to watch for trouble. The test set (another held-out slice, or your eval set from Part 5) is used once at the end for the honest score. Never let the three overlap.

Step 5 · Inside the loop

What happens during training

Epochs, batches, learning rate and loss, explained without maths, plus a live chart where you can watch overfitting happen.

Training is a loop that repeats one small step millions of times. Take a batch (a handful of examples, say 8 or 32). Show the model the prompts and let it produce answers. Measure the loss: a single number saying how far the model's answers are from the ideal answers in the batch. High loss means very wrong; zero would mean perfect. Then compute the gradient, which is the direction to nudge every weight so the loss goes down, and nudge each weight a little in that direction. The size of the nudge is the learning rate. Then take the next batch.

One pass over the entire training set is an epoch. Fine-tuning usually runs for a few epochs, often between one and five. Too few and the model has not absorbed the habit (underfitting). Too many and it memorises the exact training examples and gets worse on anything new (overfitting).

The training loop. The dot is one batch making its way round. The loss is the only feedback the model ever gets; it never sees a score, a rubric or a user.

Analogy

Learning to throw darts blindfolded, with a friend calling out how far you missed. Each throw is a step. "Two inches high" is the loss. Adjusting your arm slightly downward is the gradient. How much you adjust is the learning rate: tiny adjustments take forever, huge ones overshoot and you end up worse. Going through your whole bag of darts once is an epoch.

Watching for overfitting

During training you track two losses. The training loss is measured on the batches the model is learning from, so it almost always keeps going down. The validation loss is measured on the held-out validation set, which the model never trains on. As long as both go down, the model is learning something general. When the validation loss starts to rise while the training loss keeps falling, the model has begun memorising. Early stopping means keeping the version of the model from the epoch where validation loss was lowest and throwing away the rest.

Playground

Loss curve explorer

A simulated training run. Drag the sliders. Watch where the validation curve stops following the training curve; that turning point is where you should stop.

Key idea

Training loss tells you the model is learning the examples. Validation loss tells you whether it is learning the task. Only the second one matters, and the gap between them is your overfitting alarm.

Common doubt

"Should I just train for more epochs to be safe?" No. More epochs past the turning point make the model worse on new inputs, not better. When in doubt, train for fewer epochs and rely on early stopping.

Go deeper: the settings you will actually see

A typical fine-tuning job asks for four or five numbers: the number of epochs, the batch size, the learning rate, and for LoRA the rank (Step 3) and a scaling factor. Sensible defaults exist for all of them, and providers pick reasonable values for you. The rule for beginners: change one setting at a time, keep the validation set fixed, and write down the result. Learning rate is the one that most often needs adjusting; if the loss curve is jumpy, lower it; if it barely moves, raise it.

Step 6 · Proof

Checking the result

A finished training run proves nothing. Learn the three comparisons that tell you whether the fine-tune was worth it.

Part 5 taught you to build an eval: a fixed set of test inputs with a way to score the outputs. That eval set is the whole reason you can judge a fine-tune at all. Run it on the base model before training and on the fine-tuned model after training, with identical inputs and identical scoring. The difference between the two numbers is the effect of the fine-tune.

Three comparisons, in order

1

Before versus after, on your task

Same eval set, same grader. Did the score go up? By how much? Look at the failures the fine-tuned model still makes; they show you what the training data was missing.

2

Against the honest baseline

The baseline is the base model with the best prompt you could write, not with a lazy prompt. If the well-prompted base model scores the same as the fine-tune, you have paid for nothing. This comparison is skipped surprisingly often.

3

Regressions elsewhere

A regression is something that used to work and now does not. Run a small set of general tasks (summaries, simple maths, a different format) on both models. A fine-tune that improves your task but breaks everything else may still be a bad deal.

The failure behind comparison 3 has a name: catastrophic forgetting. When a model is trained hard on one narrow task, the weights that handled other tasks get overwritten. The model gets better at answering support tickets and worse at, say, writing a summary or following a new instruction. LoRA with a modest learning rate and few epochs makes this less likely, but it does not make it impossible, so you measure.

Key idea

If a better prompt gets the same result, do not fine-tune. The eval set decides, not the feeling that the fine-tuned model "seems better". Numbers before, numbers after, numbers on the baseline, numbers on general tasks.

# compare.py: the honest comparison, in plain Python
import json

def call_model(prompt: str, model: str) -> str:
    # Put any LLM API call here. "model" is a base or fine-tuned model name.
    ...

def load_jsonl(path: str) -> list:
    # one JSON object per line; skip blank lines
    with open(path) as f:
        return [json.loads(line) for line in f if line.strip()]

def grade(output: str, expected: str) -> bool:
    # the simplest grader from Part 5; swap in a judge for open-ended tasks
    return output.strip().lower() == expected.strip().lower()

def score(model: str, template: str, items: list) -> float:
    hits = 0
    for item in items:
        out = call_model(template.format(q=item["prompt"]), model)
        hits += grade(out, item["response"])
    return hits / len(items)

eval_set = load_jsonl("eval.jsonl")      # never used for training
general  = load_jsonl("general.jsonl")   # unrelated tasks, to catch forgetting

plain = "Q: {q}\nA:"
best  = "You are a support agent. Answer in one sentence and ask for the order number.\nQ: {q}\nA:"

print("base, plain prompt   ", score("base-model", plain, eval_set))
print("base, best prompt    ", score("base-model", best,  eval_set))  # the honest baseline
print("fine-tuned, plain    ", score("my-model-v1", plain, eval_set)) # did the habit stick?
print("fine-tuned, general  ", score("my-model-v1", plain, general))  # any regressions?
print("base, general        ", score("base-model", plain, general))
Common doubt

"The validation loss went down nicely. Isn't that enough?" No. Loss measures how closely the model's text matches the training answers word by word. It does not measure whether the answers are correct, safe or useful. A model can reach a low loss by copying your formatting quirks perfectly while getting the substance wrong. The eval set measures what you care about; the loss measures what the optimiser cares about.

Practical tip

Save the outputs of every comparison, not just the scores. Reading twenty side-by-side answers from the base model and the fine-tune teaches you more about what the training did than any single number.

Step 7 · Into production

Serving a fine-tuned model

Training makes a file. Somebody still has to run it. Two ways to do that, and what quantization and versioning mean for you.

Hosted fine-tuning is the easy route. You upload your JSONL file to a model provider, they run the training on their hardware, and when it finishes you get a new model name. Your application calls that name instead of the base model's name and nothing else changes. You never see a GPU. The trade-offs: you can only fine-tune the models that provider offers, you pay their per-token price for the new model, and your training data leaves your building.

Self-hosting means you run the model yourself. You need GPUs (rented by the hour or owned) and an inference server: a program whose job is to load the model into GPU memory and answer requests quickly, batching many users together. Tools such as vLLM are inference servers built for this. Self-hosting works only with open-weight models, models whose weight files you are allowed to download and run. Self-hosting gives you control, privacy and often a lower cost at high volume, in exchange for operating a service.

Hosted: upload data, get a model name. Self-hosted: run an open-weight base model plus your adapter on your own GPUs behind an inference server.

Merging adapters, and why quantization matters here too

An adapter from Step 3 can be served two ways. You can merge it into the base model, which means folding the adapter's small changes into the big weight grid once, producing a single ordinary model file that runs at full speed. Or you can keep the base model loaded and attach the adapter at request time, which lets one server hold one base model and dozens of adapters, switching between them per customer. Merging is simpler; loading per request is more flexible.

Quantization matters at serving time even more than at training time. A model stored with 4 bits per weight instead of 16 needs a quarter of the memory, fits on a cheaper GPU, and produces tokens faster because less data moves around. The price is a small, measurable loss of quality. Run your eval set on the quantized model too, because "small" is not "zero".

Analogy

Hosted fine-tuning is a restaurant kitchen cooking your recipe: you hand over the recipe, they buy the equipment, and you order plates. Self-hosting is building your own kitchen: more work, but nobody else touches your ingredients and the per-plate cost drops once you are busy every night.

Practical tip: version models like code

Give every fine-tune a name with a number (support-v3), and store with it the exact training file, the settings used, the base model name and the eval scores. When something goes wrong in production, you want to roll back to support-v2 and know precisely what changed. A model whose training data you cannot find is a model you cannot fix.

Key idea

Start hosted if you can; it removes every operational problem except the ones that matter (data, evals). Move to self-hosting when privacy rules, cost at volume, or the need for a model the providers do not offer forces you to.

Step 8 · Judgement

Cost, time, and when it is worth it

The effort ladder, the reasons that justify climbing it, the reasons that do not, and the mistakes almost everyone makes once.

The effort ladder. Climb one rung at a time, and only when the rung below has been tried properly and measured.

Good reasons to fine-tune

  • A strict output format on a huge volume of requests (always valid JSON, always the same fields)
  • A specific voice or house style that prompts keep drifting from
  • A small, fast model for one narrow task, distilled from a big one
  • Cutting latency and cost by shrinking the prompt
  • Classification or tagging at scale, where you have thousands of labelled examples

Bad reasons to fine-tune

  • Wanting the model to know facts (use RAG)
  • A one-off or rarely repeated task
  • No eval set yet, so no way to know if it helped
  • "The prompt is getting long" when you have not tried a tidier prompt
  • Because it sounds more serious than prompting

Common mistakes

  1. Skipping the prompt baseline. The fine-tune "wins" against a lazy prompt, and nobody checks whether a good prompt would have won too.
  2. Training on the eval set. The scores look wonderful and mean nothing, because the model has seen the exam.
  3. Inconsistent examples. Three people wrote the ideal answers in three styles, and the model learned to pick one at random.
  4. Too many epochs. The training loss looked great; the validation loss was never checked; the model recites training examples word for word.
  5. Forgetting to check general ability. The support model now refuses to write a summary.
  6. Baking in things that change. Prices, policies and product names go into the training data, then change a month later, and now the model is confidently out of date.
  7. Losing the recipe. No record of which data and settings produced the deployed model, so it cannot be reproduced or fixed.
Analogy

Hiring for a post office counter. Prompting is a clear checklist taped to the desk. RAG is the binder of current rates the clerk looks up. Fine-tuning is sending the clerk on a training course. You would not send someone on a course to learn this week's stamp prices; you would update the binder. You would send them on a course to learn how to handle every customer the same calm way.

Key idea

Fine-tuning is worth it when three things are true at once: the cheaper levers have been tried and measured, the need is about behaviour rather than knowledge, and the volume or the constraints make the training cost pay for itself. If any one is missing, wait.

Common doubt

"Do I need to be a machine learning expert to fine-tune?" Not for hosted fine-tuning with LoRA and a few hundred examples: the provider handles the maths. What you do need is the discipline from Part 5: a clean eval set, a held-out validation set, and the habit of comparing numbers rather than impressions.

Step 9 · Check yourself

Quiz and glossary

Six questions to test the ideas, then every term from this guide in one place.

Glossary

Adapter
A small set of trainable layers attached to a frozen base model. LoRA produces adapters. They can be swapped, shared and merged.
Base model
The model as it comes out of pre-training, before any fine-tuning for your task.
Baseline
The score you compare against. For fine-tuning, the honest baseline is the base model with the best prompt you can write.
Batch
A small group of training examples processed together in one training step.
Catastrophic forgetting
When training on a narrow task overwrites the weights that handled other tasks, so the model gets worse at things it used to do.
Continued pre-training
Feeding the model a large amount of raw text from a field so it absorbs vocabulary and some knowledge. Expensive and rarely needed.
Distillation
Fine-tuning a small student model on answers written by a large teacher model, to get a cheaper, faster model for a narrow task.
DPO
Direct preference optimisation. A preference-tuning method that learns directly from pairs of better and worse answers, with no separate reward model.
Early stopping
Keeping the version of the model from the epoch where validation loss was lowest, and discarding later epochs.
Epoch
One complete pass through the training set.
Eval
A repeatable test that scores a model on a fixed set of inputs (Part 5). The tool that decides whether a fine-tune helped.
Fine-tuning
Continuing the training of an already-trained model on your own examples so its behaviour shifts toward them.
Frozen weights
Weights that are not allowed to change during training. In LoRA, the whole base model is frozen.
GPU
Graphics processing unit. The chip that does the heavy arithmetic of training and running models.
Gradient
The direction to nudge each weight so the loss goes down.
Hosted fine-tuning / self-hosting
Hosted: a provider trains and serves the model on their hardware, and you call a new model name. Self-hosting: you run the model on GPUs you control, behind your own inference server.
Inference server
Software that loads a model onto GPUs and answers requests efficiently, batching many users. vLLM is one example.
JSONL
A text file format with one JSON object per line. The usual format for training data.
Learning rate
How big each weight nudge is during training. Too low is slow; too high overshoots and becomes unstable.
LoRA
Low-rank adaptation. A method that freezes the base model and trains small adapters instead, cutting memory and cost.
Loss
A single number measuring how far the model's outputs are from the ideal outputs. Training tries to push it down.
Merge (adapter merge)
Folding an adapter's changes into the base model's weights to produce one ordinary model file.
Open-weight model
A model whose weight files can be downloaded and run on your own hardware.
Overfitting / underfitting
Overfitting: the model memorises its training examples and gets worse on new inputs, seen as validation loss rising while training loss falls. Underfitting: the model has not learned enough, and both losses stay high.
Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT)
Any method that fine-tunes by changing only a small fraction of the weights. LoRA and QLoRA are the common ones.
Preference tuning
Training from pairs of answers marked better and worse, so the model learns judgement and taste rather than just format.
Pre-training
The original, enormous training run on general text that produces the base model.
QLoRA
LoRA with the frozen base model stored in quantized (compressed) form, so it fits on smaller hardware.
Quantization
Storing weights with fewer bits each. Smaller and faster, with a small loss of quality.
RAG
Retrieval-augmented generation. Looking up relevant documents at request time and putting them in the prompt (Part 3).
Regression
Something that used to work and now does not, after a change.
RLHF
Reinforcement learning from human feedback. Preference tuning that first trains a reward model to score answers, then trains the language model to score highly.
Supervised fine-tuning (SFT)
Fine-tuning on pairs of input and ideal output. The most common and simplest kind.
Synthetic data
Training examples written by a model rather than a person. Fast, but inherits the model's mistakes and blandness.
Teacher model / student model
In distillation, the large model that writes the answers (teacher) and the small model that learns from them (student).
Token
The small piece of text (roughly a word or part of a word) that a model reads and writes one at a time, and that providers bill by.
Test set
Held-out examples used once, at the end, for the honest final score. Often the same as your eval set.
Training set
The examples the model actually learns from.
Validation set
Held-out examples checked during training to watch for overfitting. Never trained on.
Weights / parameters
The billions of numbers inside a model that decide how input text becomes output text. Training adjusts them.
